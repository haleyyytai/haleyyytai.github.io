I found a hammock on Rice campus today.

I have a weakness for hammocks. Clean or not, as long as it isn't damp, I'll sit right in it.

Is my love of hammocks something atavistic?<br>
A modern person who has forgotten how to climb trees, trying to live in one anyway?

I think it might be because my grandmother had a hammock when I was little, and we always took it with us on every outing.

My cousin and I would take the hammock to the park and waste an afternoon.<br>
Digging in the dirt, of all things, in a city plagued by sandstorms.

I can never dig with that kind of abandon again. I'm a bit of a clean freak now.

I still remember digging in the dirt outside my grandmother's garage.<br>
That summer my cousin and I became obsessed with building traps. Every day we ran downstairs to hone our hole-digging technique and our camouflage skills.

We built frames out of twigs; I went around like a bird collecting flat pieces of trash. My favorite was torn-up chip bags (non-biodegradable waterproof material!). Then we covered it all with leaves and sprinkled dirt on top to disguise it as level ground.

I also very creatively proposed sticking twigs upright in the hole to increase the lethality.<br>
Luckily my cousin was a little older than me and stopped me — apparently she didn't have my villain talent.

In the end we'd gotten a little too good at it: my grandfather, coming home from work, twisted his ankle in our trap.<br>
It was so stupid. Who digs a trap right outside their own front door?

I no longer remember how old I was. Seven or eight, probably. Second or third grade. I never felt like I had a best summer; the other summers I simply can't remember now.<br>
The short haircut my mom took me to get was, thinking back, hideous. Mercifully, back then I couldn't have cared less what I looked like.

What was I thinking, lying in that hammock that summer?<br>
It's a shame I don't remember.

If there was food, I ate it; if there were clothes, I wore them; if there was somewhere to go, I went. Living some kind of unexamined life.<br>
Somewhere, Socrates rolls his eyes.

Later that summer I devoted myself to improving my grasshopper-catching skill. I didn't even like bugs. I was a little afraid of them.<br>
I was afraid of them while I caught them, and afraid of my own hands after.<br>
This went on until my other grandmother, looking at the kitchen filling up bottle by bottle with animals that weren’t on our family’s menu, set them free one by one while urging me to stop sinning.

It took my parents a while to realize those weren't even our bottles. Their little scientist had been hunting grasshoppers around the park and picking up discarded plastic bottles to store the surplus.

Environmentally responsible, ethically questionable when it came to animal welfare.<br>
At least they were pests.

After that I became obsessed with foraging for wild greens, in that very same park, and converted from fauna to flora.<br>
That summer the whole family dined on weeds I personally refused to eat.

For a while I had the hobbies of a wild animal. When I announced that I wanted to be an archaeologist, my mom wasn't worried about my practically nonexistent memory for anything in the humanities. She worried that digging things up outdoors might be too tiring.<br>
She knew how much I loved digging.

These days I sit in an office, tapping at a keyboard.<br>
Those scenes feel like something I read somewhere. Good thing I didn't grow up in Hulan, or I'd think I hallucinated them out of *Tales of Hulan River*. I can't even be sure the main character of that story is me, but what other reason would I have for knowing a life in such detail?

Despite the looming CVPR deadline, here I am, writing a memoir about catching grasshoppers. It's more important right now.<br>
Maybe one day, in another hammock, I'll remember today.
